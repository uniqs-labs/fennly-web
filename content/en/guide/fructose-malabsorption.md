In everyday use, fructose intolerance almost always means fructose malabsorption: your small intestine can only absorb so much fruit sugar, and what is left over is fermented by gut bacteria in the large intestine, which can cause gas, bloating, tummy pain or diarrhoea [1] [6]. A doctor can look into it with your history, a food and symptom diary and often a hydrogen breath test [1] [6] [10]. What follows is not a lifelong ban on fruit but a short phase of eating less fructose and then finding out how much you personally tolerate [6]. Keep this apart from hereditary fructose intolerance, a rare inherited condition that shows up in infancy and can damage the liver and kidneys [1] [2].

## Fructose intolerance or fructose malabsorption: what's the difference?

Fructose is fruit sugar. It occurs naturally in fruit, some vegetables and honey, and it is also made from corn as high fructose corn syrup, which is used in many sweeteners and soft drinks [6]. The term fructose intolerance is used for two very different things:

- **Fructose malabsorption (dietary fructose intolerance):** the common form. Fructose is absorbed in the small intestine only up to a limit; what is not absorbed reaches the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it into short chain fatty acids and gases [1] [6].
- **Hereditary fructose intolerance:** an inherited metabolic disease in which the liver lacks an enzyme needed to break down fructose. It is rare: MedlinePlus estimates its incidence at about 1 in 20,000 to 30,000 [2].

This guide is about the common form, fructose malabsorption. The hereditary form has its own section below, because completely different rules apply.

One point that often gets lost: not being able to absorb unlimited fructose is normal. The European breath test guideline notes that the gut's capacity to absorb fructose and sugar alcohols such as sorbitol is limited, and large amounts are incompletely absorbed even in healthy people [1]. Carbohydrate malabsorption is not in itself abnormal; it usually only matters if it causes symptoms, and then it is called an intolerance [1].

## What are the symptoms of fructose malabsorption?

Symptoms are in the gut. People with fructose or fructan intolerance often report [6]:

- Bloating and a swollen tummy (distension)
- Gas and wind
- Tummy pain
- Diarrhoea
- Belching
- Nausea

Why these symptoms? Unabsorbed fructose draws water into the bowel and is then fermented in the large intestine. The extra water can lead to diarrhoea, and the gases can contribute to bloating, pain and wind [1] [6]. According to the European guideline, whether someone gets symptoms depends on many factors: the amount eaten, the gut bacteria and how sensitive the gut is. That is why people react very differently, and even the same person does not react the same way every day [1].

These symptoms are not specific. According to the European guideline, lactose and other FODMAPs (poorly absorbed, fermentable carbohydrates) cause symptoms through the same mechanisms as fructose [1]. Suspecting fructose is not a substitute for seeing a doctor.

## How much fructose can you absorb?

Studies have tested how much fructose the gut can handle at once, using pure fructose dissolved in water. In a double-blind study of 20 healthy adults, no breath test was positive after 15 g of fructose, 2 of 20 were positive after 25 g (without symptoms), but 16 of 20 were positive after 50 g, and 11 had symptoms [3]. In an older study of 103 healthy people, 58% did not fully absorb 50 g of pure fructose, and about half of them had abdominal symptoms [4].

The European guideline sums it up: a dose that most people tolerate has not been studied systematically, but doses below 25 g of fructose did not cause abdominal symptoms in healthy volunteers, even in those with proven malabsorption [1].

Two things make a real difference in everyday eating:

- **Glucose helps absorption.** When glucose was taken together with fructose, incomplete absorption fell substantially [4]. In natural foods, fructose occurs together with glucose or as table sugar (sucrose) [4]. It is thought that free fructose, meaning fructose in excess of glucose, matters most for how much is malabsorbed [6] [8].
- **Real food is not a test drink.** Many studies use pure fructose in amounts and concentrations above typical intake [7]. Results from lab tests can therefore only partly be applied to a normal meal.

## Which foods are high in fructose?

These lists help you spot likely suspects. They are not a list of forbidden foods, because how much you tolerate is individual [6].

| Group | High in excess fructose or sorbitol | Low FODMAP alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit | Apples, apple juice, pears, mangoes, cherries, figs, nashi pears, watermelon, dried fruit [8] | Cantaloupe, kiwifruit, mandarin, orange, pineapple, blueberries [8] |
| Sweeteners and sweets | Honey, high fructose corn syrup, sugar-free confectionery [8] | Table sugar, maple syrup, rice malt syrup, dark chocolate [8] |

Watch out for **sorbitol** as well. Like fructose, this sugar alcohol is only partly absorbed [1]. It is common in sugar-free products, and Monash notes that apples and pears contain both fructose and sorbitol [8]. Monash advises reading labels, especially on artificially sweetened drinks and confectionery, and talking to a dietitian before starting a FODMAP diet [8].

Monash University measured FODMAP content in its lab and rates foods with a traffic light system in its app [8]. For all FODMAP groups at a glance, see our [FODMAP food list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/).

## How is fructose malabsorption diagnosed?

It starts with your GP. The NHS says you may be asked to keep a food and symptom diary to help work out what may be triggering your symptoms, and you may be referred to a dietitian [10].

If fructose is suspected, a **hydrogen breath test** is the usual test [6]. The European guideline describes it like this [1]:

- You come fasted, and the day before you avoid poorly absorbed, fermentable carbohydrates and fibre.
- You drink 20 to 25 g of fructose dissolved in water.
- Breath samples are then usually taken every 30 minutes for 3 to 5 hours.
- A rise in breath hydrogen of at least 20 ppm (parts per million) above baseline indicates malabsorption. Your symptoms are recorded at the same time, and the report should state separately whether there is malabsorption and whether there is intolerance.

The test has limits worth knowing:

- **A reading is not a symptom.** In a large single-centre study of 1,372 people with functional gut disorders, everyday symptoms matched the symptoms felt during the test, but not the measured malabsorption [5].
- **Results vary.** There is little evidence on test-retest reliability, and breath hydrogen after fructose has varied widely when the test was repeated [1].
- **Sources of error.** Bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine, very fast gut transit or poor oral hygiene can cause a false positive result [1].

For IBS, the UK guideline NICE says a hydrogen breath test is not needed to confirm the diagnosis in people who meet the IBS criteria; it refers to breath tests for lactose intolerance and bacterial overgrowth [9]. Whether a test makes sense for you is a decision for your doctor. Our guide to [food intolerance tests](/en/guides/food-intolerance-test/) explains which other tests are worth doing and which to skip.

## What helps with fructose malabsorption?

A completely fructose-free diet is cumbersome and not usually required [6]. There are no established guidelines for the exact approach, so care depends on the clinic's experience [6]. One US centre describes this approach [6]:

1. **Cut down for a short time:** about 2 weeks with around 5 g of fructose a day. Relief there usually came within 2 to 6 weeks.
2. **Reintroduce:** then bring back small amounts of foods with somewhat more fructose, one at a time, to find out how much you personally tolerate.
3. **Keep it as varied as possible:** the goal is the least restrictive diet that still keeps your symptoms under control. In that centre, patients typically tolerated 10 to 15 g of fructose a day.

Things you can discuss with your doctor or a dietitian:

- Foods with excess fructose are thought to be more likely to cause trouble than foods with a balance of fructose and glucose [4] [6].
- Read labels, especially on artificially sweetened drinks and confectionery [8].
- Fructose is not necessarily the only trigger: in IBS, FODMAPs can add up, and some people react more to some FODMAP groups than others [6].

Important: do not cut foods out of your diet without advice from a GP or dietitian [10]. For IBS, NICE says exclusion diets such as low FODMAP should only be guided by a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [9].

When you reintroduce foods, a diary that captures amounts and delayed reactions helps; our guide on [how to keep a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/) explains how. ## Hereditary fructose intolerance: when not to experiment

Hereditary fructose intolerance is a completely different condition from fructose malabsorption [1]. It is caused by changes in the ALDOB gene, which leave the liver short of aldolase B, the enzyme that breaks down fructose [2]. Symptoms start in infancy, when fruit, juice or other fructose-containing foods are introduced: nausea, bloating, tummy pain, diarrhoea, vomiting and low blood sugar [2]. Repeated fructose intake can lead to liver and kidney damage [2]. Many people with the condition develop a dislike of fruit, juice and other foods containing fructose [2].

The European guideline stresses that fructose malabsorption must be distinguished from this hereditary disease [1]. If you have always vomited after fruit or sweet foods, or notice signs of low blood sugar after them, do not experiment with fructose; talk to your GP.
