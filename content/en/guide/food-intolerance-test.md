There is no single food intolerance test that checks for everything. The tests that work answer one specific question: a hydrogen breath test for suspected lactose or fructose intolerance, a blood test for coeliac disease, IgE allergy tests, and for many suspicions cutting out a suspected food for a limited time and then reintroducing it with professional support [1] [5] [7] [9]. IgG tests, bioresonance, applied kinesiology and hair analysis are not suitable for diagnosing food intolerance, and allergy societies advise against them [1] [2] [4].

## Food intolerance, allergy, malabsorption: what's the difference?

Before choosing a test, it helps to get the terms straight, because each test only answers one kind of question.

- **Food intolerance:** your body has difficulty digesting a food or an ingredient. Common symptoms are diarrhoea, bloating, gas and tummy pain, usually a few hours after eating. Your immune system is not involved, so an intolerance cannot cause a serious allergic reaction [9].
- **Food allergy:** here the immune system reacts. In classic food allergy the body makes IgE antibodies, immune proteins that can trigger fast reactions [1]. Itchy skin, wheezing or swelling of the lips, face and eyes point to an allergy rather than an intolerance [9].
- **Malabsorption:** a sugar such as lactose or fructose is not fully absorbed in the small intestine and reaches the large intestine. According to the European breath test guideline, this is not in itself abnormal or even unusual in healthy people. It usually only matters if it causes symptoms, and only then is it called an intolerance [5].

That last point is the heart of the matter: an abnormal test result on its own does not prove that a food causes your symptoms. This applies to breath tests just as much as to allergy tests [1] [5].

## Which food intolerance tests actually work?

### Hydrogen breath test for lactose and fructose

The hydrogen breath test is a test doctors use to diagnose lactose malabsorption and lactose intolerance [6]. It measures whether more hydrogen shows up in your breath after you drink a sugar solution. That happens when gut bacteria ferment the unabsorbed sugar in the large intestine [5].

The European guideline describes how it should be done [5]:

- Fast for at least 8 hours, and the day before avoid poorly absorbed, fermentable carbohydrates and fibre.
- Wait at least 4 weeks after antibiotics and at least 2 weeks after bowel preparation for a colonoscopy.
- For lactose you drink 25 g of lactose, for fructose 20 to 25 g of fructose. Breath samples are then usually taken every 30 minutes for 3 to 5 hours.
- Your symptoms are recorded during the test.

That last step is what counts. According to the NIDDK, a doctor may diagnose lactose intolerance if both your breath hydrogen rises and your symptoms get worse during the test [6]. The European guideline therefore says the report should state separately whether there is malabsorption and whether there is intolerance [5].

Breath tests have limits worth knowing [5]:

- **False negatives:** in at least 10% of people, gut bacteria do not produce enough hydrogen for the test to pick up.
- **False positives:** bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine, very fast gut transit or poor oral hygiene can mimic a rise.
- **Symptoms without malabsorption:** some people get symptoms during the test even though the test shows no malabsorption. Possible reasons include a false negative test, a sensitive gut or a nocebo effect, meaning symptoms that come from expecting them.

A crucial point about fructose: common fructose malabsorption is something completely different from hereditary fructose intolerance [5] [10]. The hereditary form is a rare genetic metabolic disease that starts in infancy as soon as fructose enters the diet, and continued exposure can lead to low blood sugar and liver and kidney damage [10]. If you think the hereditary form could apply to you, do not experiment with fructose; see a doctor. Our guide to [fructose malabsorption](/en/guides/fructose-malabsorption/) covers the common form in detail.

And for IBS: NICE says a hydrogen breath test is not needed to confirm a diagnosis of irritable bowel syndrome in people who meet the IBS diagnostic criteria [8]. Whether a breath test makes sense for you is a decision for your doctor.

### Blood test for coeliac disease

Whenever gut symptoms persist without explanation, coeliac disease belongs on the list too: NICE recommends offering the blood test in that situation and to adults with IBS [7]. The first-choice tests are total IgA and IgA tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibodies [7].

Timing matters. The test is only reliable while you are eating gluten. NICE advises eating some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing, and not starting a gluten-free diet, even after a positive blood test, until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [7].

### Allergy tests: IgE blood test, skin prick test, food challenge

If a true food allergy is suspected, IgE blood tests and skin prick tests are the first tests after a detailed history [1]. They show sensitisation, meaning your immune system is primed against a food. According to the guideline of the German-speaking allergy societies, a positive result only matters if matching symptoms occur, and on its own it should not lead you to cut out a food [1]. The guideline does not recommend broad screening panels without a specific suspicion [1].

The gold standard for diagnosing IgE-mediated food allergy is the double-blind, placebo-controlled food challenge [1]. Under medical supervision you are given either the suspected food or a placebo without knowing which. Because of the risk of an allergic reaction, this test belongs in specialist hands only [1].

### Elimination and reintroduction

For many suspicions, the practical route is what is left: cut out the suspected food for a while, see whether you feel better, then reintroduce it on purpose. The NHS describes exactly this as a test for suspected food intolerance, together with a food and symptom diary [9].

The allergy guideline keeps a diagnostic elimination diet short on purpose. For chronic conditions such as atopic eczema, it says it should usually last no longer than 1 to 2 weeks; reactions that are not driven by IgE antibodies may need 3 to 4 weeks [1]. If your symptoms do not improve during that time, either they are not related to food or not all triggers have been removed, or other factors affect the reaction [1]. For FODMAPs, our [FODMAP food list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/) shows how the elimination and reintroduction phases work.

## Which food intolerance tests to skip

There is a growing market for tests that screen for hundreds of foods at once [2]. The guideline of the German-speaking allergy societies states clearly that these methods should not be used to diagnose food allergy or intolerance [1].

| Test | What it is | Why it is not recommended |
|---|---|---|
| IgG or IgG4 blood test | Antibodies to many foods at once | Mainly shows that you eat a food regularly; often positive in healthy people [1] [2] |
| Bioresonance, electrodermal testing | Measuring electrical skin signals | No sound basis, not validated technically or clinically [1] [4] |
| Applied kinesiology | Muscle strength test while holding a food | Not validated, not reproducible [1] [4] |
| Hair analysis, iridology | Hair sample or looking at the iris | Not validated [1] [4] |
| Cytotoxic tests (for example ALCAT) | Changes in white blood cells in the lab | Not validated [1] [4] |
| Lymphocyte transformation test with foods | Immune cell reactions in the lab | Cannot tell affected from healthy people [1] |

### Why IgG food intolerance tests mislead

IgG tests are persistently promoted [2]. They do measure something, just not what they claim. According to the report of the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI), many people have positive IgG4 results without any matching symptoms [2]. IgG4 to foods shows that the immune system has met the food repeatedly and is seen as a sign of tolerance rather than intolerance [2]. The EAACI therefore considers the test irrelevant for working up food-related complaints [2]. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) has backed this position [3].

A US review sums up the whole group of these tests: in some, the lab method itself is valid but no studies show a link with symptoms; for others, blinded controlled studies found the results are neither reproducible nor related to symptoms, and most lack biological plausibility [4].

### Home food intolerance test kits

The NHS does not recommend home tests that claim to diagnose food intolerances. There is limited evidence that they give accurate results, and they may suggest you avoid many foods, which can be harmful [9]. Cutting out a long list of foods after such a test risks missing out on vitamins and minerals [9], and you still won't know what is really behind your symptoms.

## A step-by-step way to find your trigger foods

1. **Warning signs first:** warning signs such as blood in your stool, weight loss you cannot explain, fever or symptoms that wake you at night need a doctor first. The breath test guideline also says people with alarm symptoms should have other investigations before a breath test [5].
2. **Write down food and symptoms:** the allergy guideline recommends a food and symptom diary, kept for 2 to 3 weeks if symptoms are ongoing, covering food, drinks, sweets and chewing gum along with when, where and in what situation symptoms occur [1]. Our guide on [how to keep a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/) explains how to log so that patterns show up. Fennly can help with this: you log meals by photo, voice or text, the app accounts for delayed reactions and after a few days shows likely suspects with an estimate of how certain they are, explicitly not a diagnosis.
3. **Take the diary to your GP:** they can decide which targeted test fits your suspicion, such as a breath test, a coeliac blood test or a referral to an allergy clinic [1] [6] [7]. Keep eating gluten until you have had the coeliac test [7].
4. **Cut out for a limited time, then reintroduce:** only remove what your suspicion points to, for a set period, and then bring it back on purpose [1] [9]. The NHS advises against cutting foods out without the advice of a GP or dietitian [9]. If you have ever had hives, swelling or breathing problems after a food, do not reintroduce it on your own; this belongs under medical supervision [1].
5. **Restrict only as much as you need:** if a suspicion is confirmed, the NHS advice is to avoid or cut down on that food [9]. Do this with a GP or dietitian, so your diet stays balanced [9]. For lactose, our guide to [lactose intolerance](/en/guides/lactose-intolerance/) explains how much most people tolerate.

If histamine is your suspect, different rules apply: read more in our guide on [histamine intolerance](/en/guides/histamine-intolerance/).

## Common mistakes when testing for food intolerance

- **Buying an all-in-one test.** IgG tests and similar kits produce long lists that have little to do with your symptoms [1] [2] [4].
- **Going gluten-free before a coeliac test.** This can make the result unreliable; NICE advises at least 6 weeks of eating gluten before testing [7].
- **Mistaking a test result for a diagnosis.** A positive allergy test without matching symptoms is no reason to cut out a food, and an abnormal breath test without symptoms is not an intolerance [1] [5] [6].
- **Cutting out many foods at once and for good.** You won't know what helped, and you risk nutritional gaps [9].
- **Never reintroducing.** Only a planned reintroduction shows whether a food really is the trigger [1] [9].
