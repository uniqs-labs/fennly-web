Lactose intolerance means you get symptoms such as bloating, wind, tummy pain or diarrhoea after dairy, because your small intestine cannot break down all the lactose, the natural sugar in milk [6] [7]. You usually do not need to cut out dairy completely: a systematic review found that most adults with lactose intolerance tolerate 12 to 15 g of lactose at once, roughly one cup of milk [1]. Hard cheeses and butter are naturally low in lactose [3] [10]. Whether lactose is really behind your symptoms can be checked with a hydrogen breath test that also records your symptoms, because many people who think they are lactose intolerant digest lactose normally [4] [9].

## What is lactose intolerance?

Lactose is the sugar in cow's, goat's and sheep's milk [6]. To absorb it, your small intestine needs an enzyme called lactase. In many people the small intestine makes less lactase after infancy, and levels get lower with age. This is called lactase non-persistence or primary lactase deficiency, and it is the most common cause of low lactase [8]. Symptoms may not begin until later childhood, the teen years or early adulthood [8].

Lactose that is not digested passes into the colon, where bacteria break it down and create fluid and gas. In some people, that extra fluid and gas causes symptoms [8].

Two terms are often mixed up, and the difference matters:

- **Lactose malabsorption:** your small intestine cannot digest all the lactose you eat or drink. It can be measured, but it does not always cause symptoms [7].
- **Lactose intolerance:** malabsorption that does cause symptoms. Only people who have symptoms are lactose intolerant [7].

A US National Institutes of Health consensus panel concluded that most people with lactose malabsorption do not have clinical lactose intolerance, and that many people who think they are lactose intolerant actually absorb lactose normally [4].

How common lactose malabsorption is depends a lot on where your family comes from. It is common in Africa and Asia and less common in northern Europe, where many people carry a gene that lets them keep digesting lactose [7]. You may see a figure of 68% of the world's population; it comes from a 2017 meta-analysis that the journal retracted in 2025, so we do not use it here [11].

Lactose intolerance can also be **secondary**: infections or conditions that injure the small intestine, such as coeliac disease or Crohn's disease, can make it produce less lactase. If the cause is treated, you may be able to tolerate lactose again [8]. Many people newly diagnosed with coeliac disease regain the ability to digest lactose after 6 to 12 months on a gluten-free diet [3]. If you suspect coeliac disease, get tested before you cut out gluten, because a gluten-free diet can make the test unreliable.

It is not the same as a **milk allergy**, which is an immune response to proteins in milk. A milk allergy most often appears in the first year of life and a serious reaction can be life threatening, while lactose intolerance causes uncomfortable symptoms [8].

## What are the symptoms of lactose intolerance?

Common symptoms include [6] [8]:

- tummy pain or discomfort
- bloating
- wind (gas) and tummy rumbling
- diarrhoea, or sometimes constipation
- feeling sick or being sick

According to the NHS, some people also notice symptoms outside the gut, such as headaches, tiredness and joint or muscle pain [6]. Symptoms can start a few minutes or a few hours after food or drink containing lactose [6], and they may be mild or severe depending on how much lactose you have [8].

None of these symptoms is specific to lactose. Other poorly absorbed, fermentable carbohydrates (FODMAPs) can cause symptoms through the same mechanism [2], and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and small bowel bacterial overgrowth can all feel similar [9]. That is why it is worth checking the cause before you change your diet for good [6].

## How much lactose can you have?

This is the question that matters most day to day. The research gives a useful range, not a fixed limit for everyone.

- **At one time:** a systematic review of 36 randomised trials found moderate-quality evidence that 12 to 15 g of lactose, about one cup of milk, is well tolerated by most adults [1]. In controlled, blinded studies most people with lactose malabsorption tolerated at least 12 g even without a meal [2].
- **With a meal:** when lactose is taken with other nutrients, people with lactose malabsorption usually tolerate up to 18 g without notable symptoms [2].
- **Spread over the day:** in studies, up to 70 g of lactose a day in divided doses may be tolerated without a change in symptoms compared with a low-lactose period [2]. That is a study result, not a target; your own limit may be much lower.

How wide the range is shows up in a blinded study from China in which all participants had primary lactase deficiency. Among healthy controls, 10 g of lactose rarely caused symptoms, 22% reported symptoms after 20 g and 73% after 40 g [2]. People with diarrhoea-predominant IBS were more likely to get symptoms at low and moderate doses, even though they did not absorb lactose worse [2] [3]. A few people report symptoms after as little as 3 g [2].

The NIDDK suggests some practical ways to find your level: have small amounts of milk at a time and with meals, add dairy back a little at a time and see how you feel, and try yoghurt and hard cheeses, which some people find easier to tolerate than other dairy foods [10].

## Which foods are high and low in lactose?

All dairy contains lactose, but amounts vary a lot. The figures below are representative values from a review; individual products can differ [3].

| Food | Lactose per 100 g | Lactose per typical serving |
|---|---|---|
| Whole milk | 4.7 g | 15 g |
| Goat's milk | 4.5 g | 13 g |
| Fresh yoghurt | 3.0 g | 9.3 g |
| Buttermilk | 3.0 g | 9.0 g |
| Latte macchiato | 4.3 g | 8.6 g |
| Soft ice cream | 6.4 g | 5.7 g |
| Cream | 3.6 g | 3.2 g |
| Cream cheese | 3.0 g | 0.9 g |
| Butter | 0.5 g | 0.1 g |
| Soft cheese (for example camembert) | 0.3 g | 0.1 g |
| Hard cheese (for example cheddar, gruyère) | 0.1 g | under 0.1 g |
| Lactose-free milk | under 0.1 g | under 0.1 g |

Lactose also turns up in processed foods. The NHS and NIDDK list, among others, breakfast cereals, bread and baked goods, sauces and salad dressings, processed meats, instant potatoes, soups, meal replacement and protein shakes, and some non-dairy coffee creamers [6] [10]. On ingredient lists, words such as milk, lactose, whey, curds, milk by-products, dry milk solids and nonfat dry milk powder mean the product contains lactose [10]. ## How is lactose intolerance diagnosed?

Your doctor will ask about your symptoms, family and medical history and eating habits, may examine your tummy, and may ask you to stop having milk and dairy for a while to see if your symptoms go away [9]. You can note what you eat and when symptoms start, and take these notes to the appointment.

The main test is the **hydrogen breath test**. You drink a liquid containing a set amount of lactose, then breathe into a container every 30 minutes over a few hours while you are asked about your symptoms. If both your breath hydrogen rises and your symptoms get worse during the test, your doctor may diagnose lactose intolerance [9]. The European breath test guideline recommends 25 g of lactose for testing intolerance in adults; doses of 40 to 50 g equal about a litre of milk and are not representative of a normal diet [2]. The guideline also says breath tests need a symptom assessment at the same time to establish intolerance [2]. The NHS adds that blood tests may be used, and that a gastroscopy may be needed if symptoms are severe and do not improve [6].

A genetic test can detect primary lactase non-persistence in people of European descent, but it is not advised for people of African or Asian heritage, and it cannot detect secondary lactose malabsorption [3].

If you meet the diagnostic criteria for IBS, the UK guideline NICE says a hydrogen breath test for lactose intolerance is not needed to confirm the IBS diagnosis [5]. A review in Gut notes that reacting to low or moderate doses of lactose often points to IBS, and that people in this group usually do better with a time-limited low FODMAP phase, followed by reintroduction and ideally guided by a dietitian, than with a low-lactose diet [3]. ## What helps with lactose intolerance?

For most people, symptoms can be prevented by eating smaller portions of foods that contain lactose [6]. A strict lactose-free diet is usually not needed [3]. The NIDDK suggests reducing the amount of lactose you have for a short, limited period, ideally agreed with your GP or dietitian, then adding milk and milk products back a little at a time to see how you feel [10].

Do not cut foods out for good without advice from a GP or dietitian [6]. One reason is bone health: the NIH consensus panel found that many people with real or perceived lactose intolerance avoid dairy and get too little calcium and vitamin D, which may lead to weaker bones over time [4]. Lactose-free and lactose-reduced milk products are just as healthy as regular ones, and non-dairy sources of calcium include canned salmon or sardines with soft bones, broccoli and leafy greens, almonds, dried beans, tofu and calcium-fortified foods [10].

If you notice that the amount matters more than the food itself, tracking the total over the day helps. ## When should you see a doctor?

Lactose intolerance can cause uncomfortable symptoms, while a serious allergic reaction to milk can be life threatening [8]. The NHS advises seeing a GP if your symptoms keep coming back after eating, if your bowel habits have changed for 3 weeks, if you have had bloating and discomfort for 3 weeks, or if you are losing weight without trying [6]. The full list of warning signs is below this article. Swelling of the lips, mouth or throat, a rash or trouble breathing after dairy are not signs of lactose intolerance but of a possible serious allergic reaction: call 999 in the UK, 911 in the US, or your local emergency number [6].
