There is no single IBS diet that works for everyone [9], but guidelines set out a clear order to try things in [1] [2]. Start with the basics: regular meals, no more than 3 cups of tea or coffee a day, less alcohol, fizzy drinks and fatty food, and no more than 3 portions of fresh fruit a day [1] [9]. Then adjust for your main symptom, for example oats and linseeds for gas and bloating or avoiding sorbitol for diarrhea [1]. Only if that is not enough is a time-limited low FODMAP diet with a dietitian the next step [1] [2] [6] [7].

## What IBS is and why food matters

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a common gut condition with symptoms such as tummy pain, bloating, gas and changes in bowel habit [7]. The British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) now classifies it as a disorder of gut-brain interaction, meaning a problem with how the gut and the nervous system work together [11]. The British Dietetic Association (BDA) estimates that it affects around 1 in 5 adults [3].

Food can affect symptoms, but the BDA is clear that IBS is not caused by a food allergy [3]. And what helps one person may not help the next: the NHS says there is no single diet or medicine that works for everyone with IBS [9]. That is why guidelines work in steps rather than with one fixed list.

One thing first: this advice assumes your IBS has been diagnosed by a doctor. Other conditions such as coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease should be ruled out first [3].

## Step 1: the basics guidelines recommend first

The UK guideline from NICE and the BDA guideline put general healthy eating advice first, which any healthcare professional can give [1] [2]. Low FODMAP comes later [2].

| Advice | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Eat regularly | Don't skip meals or leave long gaps, and take time to eat [1] |
| Smaller meals | Smaller meal sizes may ease symptoms [3] |
| Drink enough | At least 8 cups of fluid a day, especially water or non-caffeinated drinks such as herbal tea [1] |
| Limit caffeine | No more than 3 cups of tea or coffee a day [1] |
| Cut down on alcohol and fizzy drinks | The BDA suggests no more than 2 units of alcohol a day and at least 2 alcohol-free days a week [1] [3] |
| Less fatty, spicy and processed food | The NHS advises against eating lots of fatty, spicy or processed foods [9] |
| Limit fruit | No more than 3 portions of fresh fruit a day; a portion is about 80 g [1] |
| Less resistant starch | Often found in processed or re-cooked foods [1] |

Resistant starch is starch that is not digested in the small intestine and reaches the large intestine intact [1].

Sounds too simple? A Swedish trial in 75 people with IBS compared a similar kind of traditional advice with a low FODMAP diet for 4 weeks. The traditional group ate regular meals, avoided large meals and ate less fat, insoluble fiber, caffeine and gas-producing foods such as beans, cabbage and onions. Symptoms improved clearly in both groups, with no significant difference between them [4]. So the basics are worth doing properly. The BDA also suggests making one change at a time and keeping a food and symptom diary while you do, so you can see what helped [3].

## Step 2: adjust for your main symptom

IBS looks different from person to person. Some people mainly have diarrhea, others constipation or gas. Guidelines give different tips for each.

### For gas and bloating

- Eat oats regularly, such as porridge, and up to 1 tablespoon of linseeds (flaxseeds), whole or ground, a day [1] [9]. The BDA suggests having at least 150 ml of non-caffeinated fluid with each tablespoon [3].
- Limit foods that are harder to digest or produce gas, such as cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, beans and onions [3] [9].
- Avoid products with the sweetener sorbitol, including sugar-free mints and gum [3] [9].

If bloating is your main problem, our guide to [bloating after eating](/en/guides/bloating-after-eating/) goes into more detail.

### For diarrhea

- Avoid sorbitol, a sweetener in sugar-free sweets, gum and drinks [1]. The BDA also lists mannitol and xylitol [3].
- Try cutting down on high-fiber foods such as wholegrain bread, brown rice, nuts and seeds [9].
- Drink plenty of non-caffeinated fluid to replace what you lose, and keep caffeinated drinks to 3 a day [3].
- What else can be behind diarrhea after meals, from lactose to bile acids, is explained in our guide to [diarrhea after eating](/en/guides/diarrhea-after-eating/).

### For constipation

- Drink plenty of water to make your stool softer [9].
- Increase soluble fiber gradually; good sources include oats, pulses, carrots, peeled potatoes and linseeds [9]. A sudden increase can make symptoms worse [3]. The NIDDK suggests adding fiber slowly, by 2 to 3 grams a day, to help prevent gas and bloating [10].
- Avoid adding extra wheat bran [3].

### Which kind of fiber?

Fiber is a mixed bag with IBS. NICE advises reviewing fiber intake and adjusting it, usually down, while watching the effect on symptoms [1]. NICE discourages insoluble fiber such as bran; if more fiber is advised, it should be soluble fiber, for example from oats [1]. The NIDDK also notes that research suggests soluble fiber is more helpful for IBS symptoms [10].

## Step 3: low FODMAP as a time-limited trial

FODMAPs are carbohydrates that are hard to digest, found in foods such as apples, onions, milk, wheat and honey [10]. Our [FODMAP food list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/) shows which foods are high and low in them.

If symptoms persist despite the basics, NICE recommends further dietary steps such as a low FODMAP diet, but only with advice from a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [1]. The BDA places low FODMAP as second-line advice, to be provided by a dietitian [2]. In the US, the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends a limited trial of low FODMAP to improve overall IBS symptoms [6].

This is how Monash University describes the 3 steps [7]:

1. **Swap (2 to 6 weeks):** Replace high FODMAP foods with low FODMAP alternatives. If your symptoms do not improve, your IBS may not be sensitive to FODMAPs, and other approaches make more sense.
2. **Challenge:** Keep eating low FODMAP in the background and test one FODMAP group at a time, for 3 days each, for example milk for lactose, while you track your symptoms.
3. **Personalize:** Bring back everything you tolerate and only limit what causes symptoms, and only as much as you need to. Retest poorly tolerated foods over time, because tolerance can change.

According to Monash, about 3 in 4 people with IBS improve on the diet and about 1 in 4 do not [7]. A network meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials with 944 patients found that low FODMAP ranked first for every outcome studied and was better than BDA and NICE dietary advice for bloating [5]. The limits matter too: most trials took place in specialist clinics and did not study the effect of reintroduction and personalization [5].

## Gluten and coeliac disease: test first, then change

If bread seems to make things worse, you may be tempted to go gluten-free, and the NIDDK notes that some people with IBS have more symptoms after eating gluten even without coeliac disease [10]. Before you cut it out, though: NICE advises offering adults with IBS a blood test for coeliac disease [8], and the ACG suggests testing in people with IBS and diarrhea [6]. The test is only accurate while you are eating gluten. Eat some gluten in more than 1 meal every day for at least 6 weeks before the test, and don't start a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [8].

## How to find your own trigger foods

The NHS suggests keeping a diary of what you eat and any symptoms you get, and avoiding the things that trigger your IBS [9]. Done well, that diary is the bridge between general advice and your own gut.

- **Change one thing at a time.** Otherwise you won't know what helped [3].
- **Reintroduce what didn't help.** If cutting a food out made no difference, the BDA advises bringing it back before moving on to dietitian-led steps [3].
- **Don't keep cutting.** If you need to avoid lots of different foods to control your symptoms, see a GP, who may refer you to a dietitian [9].
- **Look for patterns, not one-off days.** Write down meals, portions and symptoms so you can spot links across several days [3] [9].

## When diet alone isn't enough

Diet is an important piece, but not the only one. The NHS also suggests finding ways to relax and getting plenty of exercise [9]. If diet changes and other treatments have not helped, the BDA says hypnotherapy or cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) may be worth trying [3], and the ACG suggests gut-directed psychotherapy for overall IBS symptoms [6]. If diet changes and pharmacy advice are not helping, see a GP, who can suggest other treatments [9].

To be honest about the evidence: even for low FODMAP, which has been tested in several randomized trials, most trials did not look at the effect of the reintroduction and personalization phase [5]. That is one more reason to watch carefully what works for you.
