Stomach pain after eating is not always a sign of anything serious, and some of it goes away after a few days on its own. Common reasons are indigestion, trapped wind, constipation and food intolerances [1] [7]. Where it hurts and when it starts give you first clues: burning or fullness in the upper tummy during or soon after a meal points more towards indigestion, tummy pain that starts a few hours later more towards a food intolerance [2] [3] [7]. Get help straight away for sudden severe pain, vomiting blood or black, sticky stools, and see a doctor if the pain does not go away or keeps coming back [1].

## When is stomach pain after eating an emergency?

Warning signs first, because they matter more than any food question. The NHS lists these situations with stomach ache in which you should call 999 in the UK (911 in the US, otherwise your local emergency number) or go to A&E [1]:

- the pain started suddenly and is severe
- you are vomiting blood, or your vomit is green or looks like ground coffee
- a lot of blood is coming from your bottom, or your poo is black and sticky
- you cannot pee at all, or cannot poo or fart at all
- you are struggling to breathe

If what feels like indigestion comes with pain in your chest, jaw, neck or arm, the US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) says to see a doctor right away [3]; call 999 (911 in the US, otherwise your local emergency number). With possible gallstones, get emergency help if the pain spreads to your back and you are being sick, if your skin or the whites of your eyes turn yellow, or if you have a very high temperature or feel hot and shivery [4].

Ask for an urgent GP appointment (in the UK you can also use NHS 111) if the pain suddenly gets much worse, there is blood in your poo, or diarrhoea or vomiting has not stopped after a few days [1]. Book a routine appointment if stomach pain, bloating or indigestion does not go away or keeps coming back, or you feel a lump in your tummy [1]. For indigestion, the NHS adds weight loss you did not intend, difficulty swallowing, being sick again and again, and iron deficiency anaemia [2].

## Where does it hurt, and when? A first map

This table does not replace an examination. It shows which causes health services and guidelines link with each pattern, so you can describe what happens more precisely to your doctor.

| What you notice | Possible causes named by the sources |
|---|---|
| Pain, burning or fullness in the upper tummy during or soon after eating, feeling full too soon | Indigestion (dyspepsia), functional dyspepsia [2] [3] |
| A painful burning feeling in the chest after eating | Heartburn, acid reflux [2] |
| Burning pain in the upper tummy that is often worse at night | Stomach ulcer [6] |
| Severe, constant pain under the right ribs or in the middle, lasting over 30 minutes, often after heavy meals and in the evening | Gallstones [4] [5] |
| Tummy pain, bloating and wind a few hours after eating, often with diarrhoea | Food intolerance [7] |
| Recurring cramps that ease after a bowel movement, with diarrhoea or constipation | Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [1] [9] |
| Bloating and a lot of wind | Trapped wind [1] |

A note on terms: the NHS uses "indigestion" narrowly (heartburn, fullness, feeling sick, burping) and says stomach ache or back pain are usually not symptoms of it; constipation is one possibility then [2]. NIDDK, by contrast, counts upper tummy pain as a symptom of indigestion [3].

## Upper stomach pain after eating: indigestion and functional dyspepsia

The upper tummy is where most after-meal discomfort shows up. Doctors call it dyspepsia: pain, burning or discomfort in the upper abdomen, feeling full too soon during a meal or uncomfortably full after a small amount, often with bloating, nausea or belching [3].

When tests find no other cause, it is called functional dyspepsia. NIDDK describes it as a disorder of gut-brain interaction, and most cases of chronic indigestion turn out to be this [3]. The international Rome IV criteria describe two forms that can overlap: one where symptoms are triggered by meals (fullness, feeling full early), and one with upper tummy pain or burning that does not only happen after eating [11]. The British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) estimates that functional dyspepsia affects about 7% of people in the community [13].

What causes it is mostly unknown. Research suggests a stomach that does not relax and expand properly with food, a small intestine that is overly sensitive to stomach acid, anxiety and depression, and genes may all play a role [3]. NIDDK also notes that specific foods are not thought to cause indigestion, although certain foods may trigger symptoms in some people with functional dyspepsia [3].

Other causes have to be ruled out first. These include peptic ulcers, infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and gut infections [3]. Some medicines can cause indigestion too, and NICE asks doctors to review medication as a possible cause [3] [8]. So do not stop anything you have been prescribed on your own; talk it through with your doctor.

### Stomach ulcer: when the pain burns

A stomach ulcer is a sore in the stomach lining, mainly caused by H. pylori infection or regular use of anti-inflammatory painkillers [6]. The pain is usually in the upper part of the tummy, may feel like burning and is often worse at night. Indigestion, heartburn, bloating and feeling or being sick can come with it [6]. Left untreated, an ulcer can lead to bleeding and other serious problems [6], which is why recurring upper tummy pain belongs with your doctor, not in a self-experiment.

## Gallstones: pain after heavy meals

Gallstones are common and most people who have them get no symptoms [4]. Pain starts when a stone blocks a bile duct. That pain, called biliary colic, may feel severe, be constant, last longer than 30 minutes and up to several hours, sit in the middle of your tummy or just under your ribs on the right, and make you feel sick or vomit [4]. According to NIDDK, these gallbladder attacks often follow heavy meals and usually happen in the evening or during the night, and after one attack more are likely [5].

The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment for tummy pain lasting longer than 30 minutes [4]. Pain lasting several hours with fever, chills or yellowing of the skin or eyes needs a doctor right away, because it can be a sign of a serious infection or inflammation [5].

## Lower stomach cramps after eating: food intolerance and IBS

### Food intolerance often shows up hours later

With a food intolerance, the NHS says you usually get symptoms a few hours after eating, and they can last for hours or days. Common symptoms are diarrhoea, bloating, farting and tummy pain, and lactose intolerance is the most common type [7]. So the meal you ate last is not always the one to blame: pain after dinner can come from lunch. If bloating is your main problem, our guide to [bloating after eating](/en/guides/bloating-after-eating/) covers it in detail.

### IBS: pain that eases after a bowel movement

In IBS, the key symptom according to NICE is abdominal pain or discomfort that is relieved by a bowel movement or comes with changes in how often you go or what your stool looks like. The pain can be anywhere in the abdomen and can move around [9].

Food matters a lot to many people with IBS. In a Swedish survey of 197 people with IBS, 84% said at least one of the foods asked about caused them symptoms. Fried and fatty foods (52%), dairy products (49%), beans and lentils (36%) and apples (28%) were among the foods named [12]. This is a single study based on self-reports: it shows what people suspect, not what has been proven to cause their symptoms. What guidelines recommend eating with IBS is in our [IBS diet guide](/en/guides/ibs-diet/).

### Test for coeliac disease before going gluten free

NICE recommends offering a blood test for coeliac disease to people with persistent unexplained abdominal or gut symptoms [10]. The test only works while you are eating gluten: some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing. Do not start a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [10].

## What can you do yourself?

Honest caveat first: there is no diet proven to work for everyone. NIDDK says specific foods are not thought to cause indigestion, though some foods may trigger symptoms in some people [3]. The steps below come from guidelines and health services.

### How and when you eat

- **Smaller meals:** the NHS advises eating smaller meals for stomach ulcer symptoms [6].
- **Take your time:** NICE advises people with IBS to have regular meals and take time to eat [9].
- **Leave a gap before bed:** the NHS suggests not eating in the 3 to 4 hours before going to bed [2]. NICE says raising the head of the bed and having your main meal well before bedtime may help some people [8].

### What you eat and drink

NICE advises people with indigestion to avoid known triggers they link with their symptoms where possible, naming smoking, alcohol, coffee, chocolate and fatty foods [8]. The NHS adds cutting down on tea and cola and avoiding rich, spicy or fatty foods [2]. Treat these as suspects to check, not a list of banned foods: not every one of them will affect you.

### Find your pattern instead of cutting out everything

When stomach pain keeps coming back, it is tempting to drop more and more foods on a hunch. The NHS warns against cutting foods out without advice from a GP or dietitian, because you could miss out on vitamins and minerals [7]. A food and symptom diary can help instead; the NHS mentions it as a way to work out triggers [7]. Note where the pain is, what it feels like and when it starts, not just what you ate: doctors ask about exactly these details [9]. Once a food looks suspicious, the usual next step is a time-limited test: you avoid it for a few weeks and then eat it again to see whether the symptoms come back, ideally with support from a dietitian [7].

## Which tests make sense?

The right test depends on the pattern. For indigestion, NICE recommends testing for H. pylori with a breath test or stool test, and treating it if found [8]. With suspected gallstones you may have an ultrasound scan and a blood test [4]. With a suspected stomach ulcer, a GP may arrange a breath, stool or blood test and may refer you for a gastroscopy, a camera test of the stomach [6]. For coeliac disease there is a blood test while you are still eating gluten [10], and for lactose intolerance a breath test [7].

The NHS does not recommend some home tests that claim to diagnose food intolerances: there is limited evidence that they give accurate results, and they may push you to avoid many foods, which can be harmful [7]. Blood tests for IgG4 antibodies to foods are specifically not recommended by the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI): these antibodies show that you have eaten a food, not that you react to it [14]. And if nothing is found, that does not mean the pain is in your head. Functional dyspepsia is diagnosed when symptoms remain unexplained after a routine evaluation [11], IBS has its own symptom pattern [9], and your doctor can help you find ways to manage both.
