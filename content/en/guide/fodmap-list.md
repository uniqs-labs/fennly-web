A FODMAP list shows which foods are high in fermentable carbohydrates, such as onion, garlic, apples, milk, legumes and wheat bread, and which are low, such as carrots, oranges, hard cheese, eggs, rice and oats [1] [2]. Serving size often decides: some foods are low FODMAP in a small portion and high in a large one [5]. The list is meant for people with medically diagnosed IBS, as a swap list for a 2 to 6 week phase followed by reintroduction, not as a permanent list of banned foods [2] [3].

## What are FODMAPs?

FODMAP stands for fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides and polyols: a group of short-chain carbohydrates (sugars) and sugar alcohols that are not completely absorbed in the gut [2]. Monash University in Melbourne, whose researchers developed the concept, groups them like this [2]:

| Group | What it includes | Typical sources |
|---|---|---|
| Oligosaccharides | Fructans and GOS (galacto-oligosaccharides) | Wheat, rye, onion, garlic, legumes |
| Disaccharides | Lactose (milk sugar) | Milk, soft cheeses, yoghurt |
| Monosaccharides | Excess fructose (fruit sugar) | Honey, apples, high fructose corn syrup |
| Polyols | Sorbitol and mannitol (sugar alcohols) | Some fruit and vegetables, sweeteners in sugar-free products |

In the small intestine, FODMAPs draw in water; in the large intestine, gut bacteria ferment them and produce gas. This happens in everyone, and most people eat high FODMAP foods every day without any trouble. In IBS, however, the gut can be more sensitive or move its contents differently, so the stretching of the gut wall is felt as pain, bloating, wind or a change in bowel habit [2]. MRI studies have shown that these carbohydrates increase water in the small intestine and gas production in the colon [12]. For most people FODMAPs cause no problems [2]. They are a possible trigger for a sensitive gut.

A note on fructose: on a FODMAP list, the issue is excess fructose that the gut does not fully absorb [1] [2]. That is completely different from hereditary fructose intolerance, a rare inherited condition. It shows up in infancy, when fruit, juice and other fructose-containing foods are first introduced, and repeated fructose intake can damage the liver and kidneys [14]. If you suspect the hereditary form, do not experiment with fructose yourself; see a doctor. How the common form shows up and how it is tested is covered in our guide to [fructose malabsorption](/en/guides/fructose-malabsorption/).

## The FODMAP food list by food group

This list is based on the examples published by Monash University, which measures the FODMAP content of foods in its own laboratory [1] [2]. Monash itself warns that many FODMAP lists on the internet are inaccurate and points to its app for complete data [1]. Treat this list as a selection of well-documented examples, not a complete database.

| Food group | High FODMAP | Low FODMAP alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetables | Artichoke, asparagus, cauliflower, garlic, green peas, sugar snap peas, leek, mushrooms, onion, red bell pepper | Eggplant, green beans, bok choy, green bell pepper, carrot, cucumber, lettuce, potato, zucchini |
| Fruit | Apples, apple juice, cherries, dried fruit, mango, nectarines, peaches, pears, plums, watermelon | Cantaloupe, green kiwifruit, mandarin, orange, pineapple, blueberries |
| Dairy and alternatives | Cow's milk, custard, evaporated milk, ice cream, soy milk made from whole soybeans, sweetened condensed milk, yoghurt | Almond milk, brie, camembert, feta, hard cheeses, lactose-free milk, soy milk made from soy protein |
| Protein | Most legumes and pulses, some marinated meat, poultry or seafood, some processed meats | Eggs, firm tofu, tempeh, plain cooked meat, poultry and seafood |
| Bread and cereals | Wheat, rye and barley breads, many breakfast cereals, biscuits and snack products | Oats, corn flakes, quinoa flakes, quinoa, rice or corn pasta, plain rice cakes, sourdough spelt bread, wheat, rye and barley free breads |
| Sugars and sweets | Honey, high fructose corn syrup, sugar-free confectionery | Dark chocolate, maple syrup, rice malt syrup, table sugar |
| Nuts and seeds | Cashews, pistachios | Macadamias, peanuts, pumpkin seeds, walnuts |

### What is behind each group

- **Fruit:** mainly sorbitol and excess fructose. Monash names apples, pears, mangoes, cherries, figs, nashi pears, watermelon and dried fruit as particularly high in excess fructose, and apples, cherries, nashi pears, peaches and plums as rich in sorbitol. Apples and pears contain both [1].
- **Vegetables:** mainly fructans, for example in artichoke, garlic, leek, onion and spring onion, and mannitol, for example in mushrooms and celery [1].
- **Grains and cereals:** mainly fructans and, to a lesser extent, GOS, for example in wholemeal bread, rye bread, muesli containing wheat, wheat pasta and rye crispbread [1].
- **Legumes and pulses:** mainly GOS, especially in red kidney beans, split peas, falafel and baked beans [1].
- **Dairy:** mainly lactose. Butter and cheese are naturally low in lactose, which is why dairy is not cut out on a low FODMAP diet [1]. How much lactose most people tolerate is covered in our guide to [lactose intolerance](/en/guides/lactose-intolerance/).
- **Meat, poultry, fish and eggs** are naturally free of FODMAPs. FODMAPs come in through added ingredients such as garlic and onion, in marinades, sausages or sauces [1].
- **Nuts:** mainly GOS and fructans; most seeds are low FODMAP [1].

A note on plant-based milks: many are low FODMAP, but they do not naturally contain calcium, so Monash advises choosing calcium-fortified versions [1].

### Hidden FODMAPs in everyday food

Much of what is on the list reaches you inside processed foods. Monash suggests reading food labels, especially on artificially sweetened drinks and confectionery, which often contain fructose or sugar polyols such as sorbitol, xylitol or erythritol [1]. Some dips, sauces and marinades contain garlic and onion, and Monash also lists vegetarian mince as high FODMAP [1]. When eating out, ask for sauces on the side if you are unsure [1]. Monash lists barbecue sauce, mayonnaise, soy sauce and chutney among the low FODMAP options [1].

## Why serving size often matters more than the food

Sorting foods into "allowed" and "banned" does not work. According to Monash, the same food can be high, moderate or low in FODMAPs depending on how much of it you eat [5]. The Monash app shows this with a traffic light system: green for low, amber for moderate and red for high, always per meal [5].

An example from a 2018 Monash article: avocado was rated red for sorbitol at a standard 80 g serving (about half an avocado), amber at 45 g and green at 30 g [5]. Take numbers like these as an illustration of the principle; the current rating in the Monash app is what counts [5].

According to Monash, it is impossible to guess the FODMAP content of a food; it has to be measured in a laboratory [2]. That takes a lot of work: Monash spends two to four weeks analysing a single food. Samples from several shops are freeze-dried, milled and measured by liquid chromatography, and then a dietitian sets the green, amber and red serving sizes [6]. Monash considers many lists found online to be inaccurate [1].

## Who is a low FODMAP diet for?

Monash is clear on this: the FODMAP diet is meant for people with medically diagnosed IBS. Many conditions cause similar symptoms, including coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, endometriosis and bowel cancer, so you should not self-diagnose IBS but see a doctor first [2]. If you are still working out where your symptoms come from, our guide to [bloating after eating](/en/guides/bloating-after-eating/) helps you sort through the common causes.

### Get tested for coeliac disease before cutting out wheat

Wheat, rye and barley are on the list because of their fructans, but they also contain gluten. NICE recommends coeliac testing for, among others, adults with IBS [13]. The test is only accurate while you are eating gluten: eat some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before the test, and do not start a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [13]. Cutting down wheat, rye and barley before the test can therefore make the result unreliable [13].

### What the guidelines say

The guidelines follow a similar order. General dietary advice comes first, such as regular meals, less alcohol and fizzy drinks, and no more than 3 cups of tea or coffee a day. Only if symptoms persist does NICE recommend exclusion diets such as low FODMAP, and only with advice from a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [7]. The British Dietetic Association also places low FODMAP as second-line advice, delivered by a dietitian [11]. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends a limited trial of a low FODMAP diet to improve overall IBS symptoms [10]. Our [IBS diet guide](/en/guides/ibs-diet/) walks through these steps in order.

## How to use the list: three steps, not a ban

Monash describes the low FODMAP diet as a three-step process, and the ACG stresses that all three phases belong together [3] [10]. A dietitian with experience in IBS should guide you through it [3].

1. **Swap (2 to 6 weeks):** replace high FODMAP foods with low FODMAP alternatives, for example wheat toast with honey for sourdough spelt toast with jam, or your daily apple for an orange [3] [4]. Monash explicitly calls this a substitution diet, not an elimination diet [4]. If your symptoms have not improved after this time, your IBS may not be sensitive to FODMAPs, and other approaches make more sense [3].
2. **Reintroduce (usually 6 to 8 weeks):** keep your background diet low FODMAP and test one FODMAP group at a time, for example by eating a food that contains only one FODMAP group daily for three days, such as milk for lactose or mango for fructose, while tracking your symptoms. Leave a few days between challenges [3] [4].
3. **Personalise:** in the end you eat everything you tolerate well again and limit only what triggers symptoms, and only as much as you need to. Tolerance can change, so it is worth retesting poorly tolerated foods after a few months [3] [4].

In step 2 you record how well you tolerated each challenge food, on paper or in an app [3]. Our guide on [how to keep a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/) explains how to log meals and symptoms so patterns actually show up. Our app Fennly can help with this: it breaks your meals down into ingredients, maps them to trigger categories such as fructans, GOS, lactose, fructose, sorbitol and mannitol, and offers a guided elimination and reintroduction test, pointing to suspects and explicitly not giving a diagnosis.

## How well does low FODMAP work, and where are the limits?

Several randomised trials support a low FODMAP diet, but the quality of those trials is limited [8] [10].

- In a randomised crossover trial from Melbourne with 30 people with IBS, participants had markedly lower symptom scores during 21 days on a low FODMAP diet than on a typical Australian diet, including less bloating, pain and wind [9].
- A 2022 network meta-analysis pooled 13 randomised trials with 944 patients. Low FODMAP ranked first for every outcome studied, although for bowel habit it was not clearly better than the other approaches, and beat the general BDA and NICE dietary advice for bloating [8].
- According to Monash, about three in four people with IBS improve on the diet, while about one in four do not [3].

The limits matter too. Most trials were run in specialist settings and did not study the effects of reintroduction and personalisation [8]. The ACG guideline notes that all published trials were judged to be at high risk of bias, and that long-term over-restriction may lead to micronutrient deficiencies [10]. A low FODMAP diet also causes marked changes in the gut microbiota; how long these last and whether they matter clinically is not yet known [12]. That is one more reason to keep the strict phase short and to reintroduce foods properly.

## Common mistakes with FODMAP lists

- **Treating the list as a permanent diet.** The goal is the least restrictive, most balanced diet that only limits your real triggers [3].
- **Starting without a diagnosis.** See a doctor first, then look at FODMAPs [2].
- **Going gluten-free before coeliac testing.** It can make the test unreliable [13].
- **Looking at the food, not the amount.** The serving size often decides between green and red [5].
- **Reintroducing everything at once.** You will not know which group caused the symptoms. Test one group after another [4].
- **Mixing lists from different websites.** Many online lists are inaccurate; rely on measured data [1].
