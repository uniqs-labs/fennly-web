An elimination diet means cutting out one suspected food or food group for a short, fixed time, checking whether your symptoms improve, and then eating it again in a planned way to see if they come back [1] [4]. The cutting out is usually short: for a suspected food allergy, 1 to 2 weeks in most cases under the German allergy guideline, and no more than 4 to 6 weeks for the strict phase of low FODMAP [1] [2]. The reintroduction is what gives you the answer [1] [5], and if nothing improves, the diet should end rather than grow [2]. Broader exclusion diets work best with a dietitian, and some people should not try them at all without medical support [2] [3].

## What is an elimination diet, and when does it make sense?

An elimination diet is a structured test, not a way of eating. The German allergy guideline describes the diagnostic version as the controlled avoidance of foods for a certain period of time [1]. The NHS describes the same idea for food intolerance: you avoid the food you might be intolerant to, see whether your symptoms get better, and after a few weeks start eating it again to see if they return [4].

It makes sense when you already have a specific suspect. A food and symptom diary is often the step before: the allergy guideline says detailed diary records can support or rule out a suspicion and help avoid unnecessary restrictions [1]. Our guide on [keeping a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/) shows what to log so a pattern can show up.

There are different kinds, and they are not equally demanding:

- **Single food avoidance:** you cut out one suspect, for example milk or onions.
- **Group diets:** you cut back a whole group, for example FODMAPs (fermentable carbohydrates that some people with IBS react to) or foods high in histamine.
- **Strict multi-food diets:** many foods at once. Avoiding many foods at once can be harmful and belongs under specialist care [3] [4].

For IBS, NICE says advice on single food avoidance and exclusion diets such as low FODMAP should only come from a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [3].

## Before you start: three checks

**1. Get tested first where a test exists.** If coeliac disease is possible, keep eating gluten until it has been ruled out or confirmed by a specialist [7]. NICE advises eating some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing [7]. The same applies to other suspicions that a proper test can check, which our guide to [food intolerance tests](/en/guides/food-intolerance-test/) explains.

**2. Rule out allergy symptoms.** If a food has ever caused hives, swelling or breathing problems, it is not something to test at home. The allergy guideline says a food challenge after a diagnostic elimination diet should take place under medical supervision, and it warns that long avoidance in a true food allergy can increase the risk of an immediate reaction when the food comes back [1].

**3. Ask whether a restrictive diet suits you.** The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) lists people who are poor candidates for restrictive diets: those who eat few of the suspected foods anyway, people at risk of malnutrition, people who cannot reliably get enough food, and people with an eating disorder or an uncontrolled psychiatric disorder [2]. Children, people who are pregnant, anyone with an eating disorder and anyone with a long-term illness should plan any diet change with their doctor.

## How to do an elimination diet step by step

1. **Pick one suspect and write it down.** Choose the food or group your diary points to. Testing several unrelated suspects at once makes the result hard to read; Monash, for example, tests each FODMAP group separately so effects do not cross over [5].
2. **Set the length before you start.** The AGA advises trying a diet for a predetermined length of time [2]. Typical time frames from guidelines and expert sources are listed in the table below.
3. **Cut out only the suspect, and replace it.** Monash points out that low FODMAP is really a substitution diet: you swap a food for a similar one, such as an orange instead of an apple or chives instead of onion [5]. That keeps your diet varied.
4. **Keep everything else as steady as you can.** Change one thing at a time [8], and keep logging food, symptoms, stress and sleep.
5. **Review at the end of the planned time.** If your symptoms clearly improved, move on to reintroduction. If they did not, stop.

|---|---|---|
| Suspected food allergy, chronic symptoms | Usually 1 to 2 weeks at most | German allergy guideline [1] |
| Reactions not driven by IgE antibodies | 3 to 4 weeks may be needed | German allergy guideline [1] |
| Low FODMAP for IBS | 2 to 6 weeks (Monash), no more than 4 to 6 weeks (AGA) | [5] [2] |
| Suspected histamine intolerance | Phase 1 of 10 to 14 days, then a test phase of up to 6 weeks; the diagnosis itself is debated | Histamine guideline [6] |

## How to reintroduce foods

Reintroduction is not an optional extra. It shows which foods really matter and keeps your diet as wide as possible [5].

- **One at a time:** Monash advises reintroducing each FODMAP group separately while the rest of your diet stays low in FODMAPs [5].
- **With a break:** leave a few days between tests so one reaction does not blur into the next [5].
- **In planned amounts:** a dietitian can advise on which food to test with, how much and in which order [5].
- **Allow time:** for low FODMAP, Monash says most people need around 6 to 8 weeks for the whole reintroduction step [5].

At the end you keep avoiding only the foods that clearly caused symptoms and bring back everything else [5]. Tolerance can change over time, so Monash suggests trying a food that did not work again after a few months [5]. The histamine guideline follows the same logic: after a short strict phase, the aim is to find your individual tolerance and move away from blanket restrictions [6]. The same guideline notes that lab tests such as DAO in blood are inconclusive and that symptoms are often not reproducible, so the condition itself is debated [6].

If your elimination diet was low FODMAP, our [FODMAP list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/) shows which foods are high and low in each group.

## How to read the results honestly

**No improvement is a result too.** If your symptoms do not improve during the elimination phase, the German allergy guideline names three possible reasons: the symptoms are not related to food, not all triggers were removed, or other factors are affecting the reaction [1]. The AGA advises dropping a diet that does not help within the planned time and trying another treatment instead [2]. Cutting out more and more foods is not the answer.

**Gluten is not always the culprit.** In a small double-blind study of 37 people with self-reported gluten sensitivity and IBS, symptoms got worse to a similar degree on gluten and on a whey protein comparison diet; gluten-specific effects showed up in only 8 percent of participants [11]. That suggests something other than gluten, possibly expectation, may play a role. This is one study, not proof for everyone, but it is a good reason to judge results over several days and, where possible, with a professional rather than after a single meal.

**One symptom-free week is not a diagnosis.** A clear pattern across elimination and reintroduction is a strong suspicion, nothing more. ## What are the risks of elimination diets?

- **Missing nutrients:** the NHS warns that cutting foods out without advice from a GP or dietitian can mean missing out on vitamins and minerals [4]. The BDA advises reintroducing foods you have cut out if your symptoms persist, and asking for a referral to a dietitian who can keep your diet nutritionally adequate [8].
- **Diets that never end:** the histamine guideline notes that many people with perceived food reactions follow extensive elimination diets for years and restrict their diet unnecessarily [6].
- **Fear of food:** some people start avoiding foods out of fear of symptoms [9], and researchers are concerned that restrictive diets may play a role in the risk of disordered eating [10].
- **Quality of life:** restrictive diets can be hard to keep up and can affect how you enjoy food and meals with others [10].

If food already feels tied up with fear or control for you, talk to your doctor before you restrict anything.

## When to get professional help

See a GP before you start if you have any of the warning signs listed below, and ask for a referral to a dietitian before you cut foods out [3] [4]. In the US, the NIDDK also notes that your doctor may suggest talking with a dietitian and that changing what you eat for several weeks may be needed to see whether IBS symptoms improve [12]. A dietitian can also help when your symptoms persist after general diet advice [8].
