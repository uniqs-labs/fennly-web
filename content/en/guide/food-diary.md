A food diary only shows useful patterns if you write things down as they happen: what you ate and drank, how much and when, plus when symptoms started, how strong they were, and how stressed and rested you felt. Food intolerance symptoms usually start a few hours after eating and can last for hours or days [1], so on a bad day you need to look back at yesterday too, not just your last meal. Keep the diary for several weeks and treat what it shows as a lead to check with a doctor or dietitian, not as a diagnosis.

## What should a food diary include?

The NHS notes that if a GP suspects a food intolerance, you may be asked to keep a food and symptom diary [1]. To show patterns, every entry needs:

- **Time:** for every meal, snack and drink. Without it, you cannot tell what came before a flare.
- **What exactly, and how much:** not "pasta" but "large bowl of pasta with tomato sauce, onion and garlic". Sauces, dressings, spice mixes, chewing gum and drinks all count.
- **Symptoms, with time and severity:** a 0 to 10 scale works well. The Salerno criteria for gluten sensitivity use the same idea, with main symptoms rated from 1 to 10 [2].
- **Bowel habits,** if they change. NICE notes that the Bristol Stool Form Scale, a chart of stool types, can help people describe them [3].
- **Stress and sleep:** a 0 to 10 stress score, hours slept, and whether the night was good or bad.
- **Anything else that varies:** infections, new medicines, alcohol, exercise, your period.

Log entries straight after eating, not from memory at bedtime: recall is unreliable and full of inaccuracies and biases [4].

## Why amounts matter more than yes or no

With many intolerances, the amount matters [5] [7]. Lactose is the classic example: a systematic review found moderate-quality evidence that most adults with presumed lactose intolerance tolerate 12 to 15 g of lactose, about one cup of milk [5]. If your diary only says "milk: yes", a splash in your coffee and a large milkshake look the same, and the pattern disappears.

The same logic applies to FODMAPs, short-chain fermentable carbohydrates found in many fruits, vegetables, dairy products, artificial sweeteners and wheat [6]. During reintroduction after a low FODMAP phase, Monash University has people eat one test food for three days in increasing amounts and record symptoms, to learn how much they tolerate [7]. For fructans, Monash even suggests separate tests with different food groups, because people often tolerate fructans from some foods but not from others [7].

So log ingredients and portions, not just dish names, and include the small stuff.

## Why symptoms can show up hours or days later

An easy mistake is to blame only the last thing you ate. The NHS says food intolerance symptoms usually appear a few hours after eating and can last a few hours or days [1]. For non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, the Salerno criteria describe a gap of hours or days between eating gluten and symptoms [2]. And Monash builds a 2 to 3 day break into reintroduction after each test so symptoms can settle [7].

Two practical rules follow:

1. On a bad day, look back over the previous 24 to 48 hours, not just at the meal right before.
2. Write down when symptoms stop, too. Bloating that lasts two days is a different pattern from an hour of discomfort after breakfast.

To be honest about the limits: none of these sources gives one time window for everyone and every food. Your own window only shows up over many days of logging.

## Stress and sleep: the columns most people skip

Food clearly matters: more than 80% of people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) report food-related symptoms [6]. But the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) guideline describes IBS, one of the most common gut disorders, as a disorder of gut-brain interaction [6]. Its symptoms come and go and are triggered by stress, other illnesses, medicines and often the act of eating [6]. Chronic stress can make symptoms more frequent and more severe [6].

Track sleep too. In a small study of 24 women with IBS, poorer self-rated sleep predicted more abdominal pain, anxiety and fatigue the next day, though not other gut symptoms [8]. That does not prove a general rule, but it is reason enough to log it.

Why this matters: if you ate a lot of bread during a stressful week with little sleep, bread will look guilty even if stress made the difference. Only when stress and sleep are logged can you tell those days apart.

## Paper food diary or app: an honest comparison

Both can work if you log close to the moment, and that is where paper has a known weakness. In a study of 80 adults with chronic pain, people using paper diaries reported making 90% of their entries on time. Hidden light sensors in the binders showed the real figure was 11%, and on 32% of days the binder was never opened even though entries existed for those days [4]. An electronic diary with audible prompts reached 94% [4]. The caveats: the study dates from 2002, it was about pain rather than food, and several authors were linked to an electronic diary company [4].

**Paper** is free, needs no account and keeps your data with you. Its weak spots: back-filling from memory, vague times and slow analysis across 48 hours.

**An app** timestamps entries, can remind you and can look for links across several days. Check where your data is stored, double-check automatically recognised ingredients, and do not expect a diagnosis.

## How to read your food diary (and what to do next)

After a few weeks:

1. **Mark the bad days:** any day whose overall score is clearly above your usual level.
2. **Look back 24 to 48 hours:** what did you eat, and how much?
3. **Compare with good days:** if the same suspect shows up in similar amounts on good days, it is less likely to be your trigger.
4. **Check the confounders:** were the bad days also the ones with high stress, poor sleep, an infection or a new medicine?

You end up with a suspicion, not proof, and it is best tested with professional help. For IBS, NICE recommends that advice on exclusion diets (diets that cut out single foods or food groups, such as low FODMAP) only comes from a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [3]. The BSG recommends that a low FODMAP diet be supervised by a trained dietitian, with FODMAPs reintroduced according to tolerance [6]. In the Monash approach, the strict phase lasts 2 to 6 weeks and reintroduction can take 8 to 12 weeks [7]. Do not cut foods out without advice from a doctor or dietitian, because you could miss out on vitamins and minerals [1]. If food already feels tied up with anxiety or control for you, talk to your doctor before restricting anything: the BSG advises identifying people at risk of overly restrictive eating before such diets [6].

### What to know before you cut anything out

Coeliac disease is an immune reaction to gluten [6]. Tests for it are only accurate while you are eating gluten: NICE advises eating some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing, and not starting a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [9]. Going gluten free first can make the result unreliable.

Skip IgG4 blood tests for food intolerance. According to the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI), these antibodies mainly show that your body has been exposed to a food repeatedly, not that you are intolerant to it, and the tests should not be done for food-related complaints [10]. The BSG also advises against elimination diets based on IgG tests in IBS [6].

Finally, if symptoms keep coming back, see a doctor [1]. A diary does not replace that appointment, but it makes the conversation far more specific.
