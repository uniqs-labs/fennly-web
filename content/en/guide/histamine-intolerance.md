Histamine intolerance is the suspicion that histamine in food triggers symptoms such as flushing, itching, nausea, diarrhoea or tummy pain [1]. Whether and how often it really occurs is unclear [1] [3]: the guideline of the German-speaking allergy societies calls the evidence for a causal link limited and contradictory, and there is no reliable test, including the blood test for the enzyme DAO [1]. Rather than following long lists of banned foods for good, the guideline recommends ruling out other causes first and then a supervised three-phase diet change with planned reintroduction [1].

## What is histamine intolerance?

Histamine is a messenger substance your body makes itself. It is involved in many processes, including itching, widening of blood vessels and the production of stomach acid [1]. Histamine is also found in food. The body breaks it down in two ways: with the enzyme diamine oxidase (DAO), made mainly in the gut, kidneys and placenta, and with histamine N-methyltransferase (HNMT), which is found in most body tissues [1].

The usual explanation goes like this: if you have too little DAO, you cannot break down histamine from food fast enough, and you get symptoms [3]. The term "histamine intolerance" was deliberately modelled on "lactose intolerance", where an enzyme deficiency is proven [1]. For histamine, that proof is still missing. The guideline states that no controlled studies show that a lack of DAO causes the symptoms, which is why it only speaks of "adverse reactions to ingested histamine" [1]. Even a Spanish review that supports the DAO idea says more evidence is needed to define, diagnose and manage the condition [3].

Histamine poisoning is a different thing. Very large amounts of histamine, mostly from spoiled fish such as tuna or mackerel, make anyone ill [1] [3]. Mild poisoning has been described from about 100 mg of histamine [1]. That is an overdose, not an intolerance [1].

## What symptoms are linked to histamine intolerance?

Because histamine acts in many parts of the body, a wide range of symptoms has been linked to it. According to the guideline, the classic ones are sudden facial flushing plus itching and redness on the body. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and tummy pain are also described. Breathing and circulation symptoms, such as low blood pressure, dizziness or a racing heart, are rarer [1].

The gut plays a big part. In a survey of people diagnosed with histamine intolerance, cited by two reviews, 92% reported bloating, and 55% to 73% each reported fullness after meals, diarrhoea, tummy pain and constipation [3] [6]. If bloating is your main problem, our guide to [bloating after eating](/en/guides/bloating-after-eating/) covers the most common other causes.

The catch: none of these symptoms is unique to histamine. A Slovak review describes how symptoms in the same person can vary a lot after similar triggers, which is exactly what makes a diagnosis so hard [5]. The guideline therefore lists other conditions that can be behind the same symptoms [1]:

| Symptom | Other possible causes, per the guideline |
|---|---|
| Flushing | mastocytosis (a build-up of certain immune cells) and other rare conditions |
| Itching | hives (urticaria) and other skin conditions |
| Nausea, vomiting | stomach or duodenal ulcer |
| Diarrhoea, tummy pain | inflammatory bowel disease, lactose intolerance, fructose malabsorption, coeliac disease, mastocytosis |
| Runny nose, breathlessness | allergic and non-allergic rhinitis, asthma |
| Low blood pressure, dizziness, racing heart | anaphylaxis (a severe allergic reaction), mastocytosis |

Timing matters too. A food intolerance is only a plausible suspect if symptoms are linked in time to eating, from minutes up to about 4 hours [1].

### Two things to rule out before any self-experiment

- **Coeliac disease:** Because coeliac disease is on the list of possible causes [1], get tested before you cut out gluten. The test only works while you are still eating gluten: the UK NICE guideline on coeliac disease advises eating some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing [9].
- **Fructose:** Fructose malabsorption, where the gut does not fully absorb fruit sugar, is not the same as the rare inherited condition hereditary fructose intolerance. The inherited form shows up in infancy, and eating fructose repeatedly can damage the liver and kidneys [10]. If you suspect it, do not experiment on yourself; see a doctor.

## Which foods are high in histamine?

Histamine in food is mostly made by bacteria. Two groups can therefore contain a lot: fish and meat that are not fresh or were processed in poor hygienic conditions, and fermented foods, where bacteria are part of production [3]. The NHS gives wine and cheese as examples of foods and drinks that contain histamine [8]. Histamine is heat-stable, so heating does not break it down; that is why it stays in canned fish [4].

| Food group | What the research says |
|---|---|
| Fermented foods (aged cheese, dry-cured sausage, sauerkraut, fermented soy products) | can contain a lot of histamine; fermented foods such as cured cheese and dry-fermented sausage were the only group excluded by every low histamine diet reviewed; levels vary widely [4] |
| Wine and beer | contain histamine, but in Spanish measurements much less than other fermented foods; alcohol can strengthen histamine's effect, and every diet reviewed excludes wine and beer [4] |
| Fish | fresh fish is usually low, poorly kept fish can be high; tuna and mackerel are classic causes of histamine poisoning [1] [4] |
| Meat | usually low when fresh and handled hygienically [4] |
| Tomato, aubergine (eggplant), spinach | contain some histamine; in spinach, levels rose during storage in the fridge [4] |
| Citrus fruit, bananas | probably appear on many lists because of putrescine, another biogenic amine that might interfere with histamine breakdown in the gut [4] |
| Yeast | is on some lists, but according to the guideline contains no relevant amount of histamine [1] |

### Why histamine food lists contradict each other

Search for a histamine food list and you will find different bans almost everywhere. A research group at the University of Barcelona compared ten low histamine diets from the scientific literature. The excluded foods differed a lot from diet to diet; only fermented foods appeared on every list [4]. For just 32% of the excluded foods could the exclusion be explained by a high histamine content [4].

Many lists also ban so-called histamine liberators, foods that are supposed to release histamine inside the body. According to the guideline, there is no reliable evidence for them so far [1]. On top of that, histamine levels vary hugely within a single food: Emmental cheese has measured anywhere from under 0.1 to 2,000 mg per kg, and smoked mackerel from under 0.1 to 1,788 mg per kg [1]. How well histamine is tolerated also depends on the food it comes in; histamine in cheese, for example, is tolerated much better than histamine in spoiled fish [1]. The guideline therefore does not consider it sensible to rate foods as safe or unsafe by their histamine content alone [1].

Context counts as well. Alcohol may slow histamine breakdown because the same enzymes are involved [1]. The guideline also names certain medicines, bowel conditions (especially inflammatory ones), hormonal status and probably the gut microbiome as factors [1]. In women, more symptoms have been observed before their period [1]. If you take regular medication, do not stop it on your own; talk to your doctor.

## Why histamine intolerance tests are so unreliable

None of the tests on offer has been shown to reliably confirm or rule out histamine intolerance [1]. Here is what the guideline says about each:

- **DAO blood test:** It has no diagnostic value. Several studies found similar levels in people with symptoms and in healthy people, and in humans DAO activity in the blood says nothing about DAO activity in the small intestine [1].
- **Stool histamine:** Gut bacteria such as lactobacilli produce large amounts of histamine themselves, so high levels in stool are not a reliable sign of disease [1].
- **Blood histamine and urine methylhistamine:** Their value is disputed or doubtful; urine methylhistamine, for example, rises on a high-protein diet [1].
- **Histamine 50 skin prick test:** No confirming follow-up studies exist so far, and it says nothing about how histamine from food is broken down [1].

A histamine challenge, drinking a set dose of histamine under medical supervision, is not a standard procedure either. In an Austrian study, 39 people with suspected histamine intolerance reacted to 75 mg of histamine in an open test. When they were then retested double-blind, meaning neither they nor the staff knew whether they got histamine or a placebo, neither their main nor their secondary symptoms could be reproduced [2]. The guideline also points out that the same dose caused symptoms in half of the healthy people in another study [1].

Experts do not fully agree. Some reviews treat improvement on a low histamine diet as confirmation of the diagnosis, or see DAO deficiency as a likely mechanism [3] [5]. The German-speaking guideline, by contrast, considers a causal link between low DAO and symptoms unproven [1]. Everyone agrees there is no simple lab test [1] [3] [5].

### What to skip

- **IgG (IgG4) food intolerance tests:** The European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) explicitly advises against them. IgG4 antibodies to foods only show that you have eaten those foods repeatedly, not that you cannot tolerate them [7].
- **Home test kits:** The NHS says there is limited evidence that home food intolerance tests give accurate results, and they may lead you to avoid many foods unnecessarily [8].
- **DAO capsules:** The guideline considers their effect unproven and does not recommend them [1].

## A step-by-step approach if you suspect histamine

The guideline suggests an approach meant to move you from blanket bans to a personal solution [1]. A dietitian should guide you, because cutting out foods without advice can leave you short of vitamins and minerals [8].

1. **Rule out other causes:** Start with the conditions in the table above, such as lactose intolerance, fructose malabsorption or coeliac disease [1]. Carbohydrates such as lactose and fructose are possible causes too [1]; our [FODMAP food list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/) shows which foods are high in fermentable carbohydrates.
2. **Keep a diary:** If the suspicion remains, a food and symptom diary helps narrow down suspicious amounts and circumstances [1]. Our guide on [how to keep a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/) explains how to log so that patterns actually show. Fennly can help here: the app assigns your meals to trigger categories including histamine, takes stress and sleep into account, and with Fennly Pro offers a guided elimination and reintroduction test, pointing to suspects and explicitly not giving a diagnosis.
3. **Restriction phase (10 to 14 days):** A vegetable-based mixed diet with less histamine and other biogenic amines. Just as important, according to the guideline, is good digestion: adjusting how meals are put together along the lines of a light, balanced whole-food diet [1].
4. **Test phase (up to 6 weeks):** You deliberately bring suspected foods back and relax the restrictions. Along the way you take factors like stress, your period or medication into account and work out how much you tolerate [1].
5. **Long-term diet:** The goal is a balanced diet with a good quality of life that restricts only as much as your personal tolerance requires [1].

To be honest, this approach is based on practical experience. Controlled studies showing how well it works are still missing [1]. For low histamine diets in general, there are only a few observational studies and no randomised trials [1]. According to the guideline, the diet change is also meant to be able to rule the suspicion out [1]: if your symptoms do not improve during the restriction phase, that speaks against histamine as the main trigger, and it is worth looking for other causes with your doctor.

## Common mistakes with suspected histamine intolerance

- **Staying on a strict diet for years.** This is exactly what the guideline warns against: many people restrict themselves far more than their symptoms justify, and it affects their social life too [1].
- **Following any histamine list you find online.** The lists contradict each other, and some ban foods that contain hardly any histamine [1] [4].
- **Trusting a DAO blood test.** A low result proves nothing, and a normal one rules nothing out [1].
- **Looking only at the food, not the context.** Amount, freshness, alcohol, stress and your cycle can decide whether you tolerate something [1].
- **Skipping other causes.** Lactose intolerance, fructose malabsorption and coeliac disease cause similar symptoms and can be tested for specifically [1].
