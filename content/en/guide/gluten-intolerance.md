Stomach aches, bloating, wind or diarrhoea after foods made with wheat, barley or rye can come from coeliac disease, a wheat allergy, non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, or another condition such as IBS or lactose intolerance [1] [9] [10]. The single most important step: get tested for coeliac disease before you cut out gluten, because the tests are only accurate while you are still eating it [2] [3]. If coeliac disease is ruled out, look closer. Studies suggest that for many people who feel sensitive to gluten, fermentable carbohydrates in wheat or the expectation that gluten will make them ill play a big part, rather than gluten itself [5] [6].

## What does gluten intolerance mean?

Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye. It is in pasta, cakes, breakfast cereals, most types of bread, certain sauces and some ready meals, and most beers are made with barley [1]. Oats do not contain gluten, but they can be contaminated if they are produced in the same place as wheat, barley and rye [1].

Three different conditions can cause symptoms after gluten or wheat, and they are handled very differently [4] [9]:

| | Coeliac disease | Wheat allergy | Non-coeliac gluten or wheat sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| What happens | Autoimmune reaction: gluten causes damage to the small intestine [1] [9] | The immune system reacts to wheat [9] | Symptoms after gluten or wheat, without damage to the small intestine [9] |
| Typical timing | Varies; some people have no noticeable symptoms [1] | Can start straight away, or days later [11] | Symptoms improve when gluten is removed and return soon after it is eaten again [4] |
| How it is diagnosed | Blood test, usually followed by a biopsy of the small intestine [2] [10] | Skin-prick or blood tests, or a trial diet without the food, often via a specialist [11] | No specific test; coeliac disease and wheat allergy must be ruled out first [4] [5] |
| What helps | A gluten-free diet, which should help control symptoms and prevent long-term complications; there is no cure [1] | Not eating the food you are allergic to, with a plan from your specialist [11] | A dietary approach that balances symptoms, nutrition and psychological factors [5] |

Experts now often call the third condition non-coeliac wheat sensitivity rather than non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, because it is not clear that gluten is the trigger [4] [5].

## What are the symptoms of gluten intolerance?

In **coeliac disease**, eating gluten can trigger gut symptoms such as diarrhoea, stomach aches, bloating and wind, indigestion and constipation [1]. It can also cause more general symptoms, including tiredness from not getting enough nutrients, unintentional weight loss, an itchy rash called dermatitis herpetiformis, nerve damage, and problems with coordination, balance and speech [1]. Even with mild symptoms, a gluten-free diet is still recommended once coeliac disease is diagnosed, because continuing to eat gluten can lead to serious complications, such as weakened bones (osteoporosis) and anaemia [1].

In **non-coeliac gluten or wheat sensitivity**, people report symptoms similar to irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), such as bloating and diarrhoea, plus symptoms outside the gut such as tiredness and headache [4]. Unlike coeliac disease, it does not damage the small intestine [9], and the European guideline notes that it is not linked to severe malabsorption [4].

A **wheat allergy** is an immune reaction to wheat. Some of its symptoms, such as itchy eyes or trouble breathing, are different from coeliac disease, and it does not cause long-term damage to the small intestine [9]. Food allergy symptoms can include itchy skin or hives, swelling of the lips, face and eyes, wheezing or breathlessness, feeling sick, tummy pain and diarrhoea [11].

The gut symptoms overlap a lot. Doctors do not diagnose coeliac disease from symptoms alone, because some of them are like those of IBS or lactose intolerance [10]. If bloating, wind or diarrhoea after meals is your main problem, it is worth looking at other fermentable carbohydrates too; our [FODMAP list](/en/guides/fodmap-list/) shows which foods are high and low in them.

## Why you should not go gluten free before testing

Going gluten free before testing can make the diagnosis harder to confirm [3]. NICE, the UK guideline body, says that any coeliac test is accurate only if you eat a gluten-containing diet during the diagnostic process, and that you should not start a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis, even if the blood test is positive [3]. The NHS gives the same advice [2], and the NIDDK in the US also advises against starting a gluten-free diet before testing because it can affect the results [10].

How much gluten, and for how long?

- **NICE:** if you are on a normal diet, eat some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing [3].
- **European guideline (ESsCD, 2025):** if you have already cut down or cut out gluten, a gluten challenge is needed. At least 3 g of gluten a day for 6 weeks is described as a balance between accuracy and discomfort, and higher doses or longer periods make the result more precise if tolerated [4]. The guideline also says the best dose and duration still need more research [4].

If you have already cut out gluten and do not want to reintroduce it, NICE advises referral to a gastrointestinal specialist and warns that the diagnosis may then be hard to confirm [3]. A genetic test can help in some cases: if you do not carry the gene variants HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8, you are very unlikely to have coeliac disease [10]. It cannot confirm coeliac disease, though, because most people with these variants never develop it [10].

## How is coeliac disease diagnosed?

Testing starts with a blood test for antibodies that are usually present in people with coeliac disease [2]. NICE recommends total IgA and IgA tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibodies as the first choice [3]. If antibodies are found, your GP will refer you to a gastroenterologist, who may arrange more blood tests or a biopsy of the small intestine, taken during an endoscopy [2]. Under the 2025 European guideline, a biopsy may be skipped for selected adults under 45 whose IgA anti-TG2 level is at least 10 times the upper limit of normal, confirmed in a second blood sample [4]. It is also possible to have coeliac disease without these antibodies, so if symptoms continue after a negative blood test, your GP may still refer you to a specialist [2].

NICE recommends offering a coeliac blood test to people with, among other things, persistent unexplained gut symptoms, prolonged tiredness, unexpected weight loss, severe or persistent mouth ulcers, unexplained iron, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, type 1 diabetes or autoimmune thyroid disease at diagnosis, IBS in adults, and first-degree relatives of people with coeliac disease [3].

Coeliac disease affects at least 1 in every 100 people in the UK, and some experts think this may be an underestimate, because milder cases may go undiagnosed or be mistaken for other conditions such as IBS [1]. ## Gluten sensitivity: what the research actually shows

Many people feel better without gluten even though they do not have coeliac disease. According to a 2025 review in The Lancet, about 10% of adults worldwide report gluten or wheat sensitivity, but in controlled challenge studies, meta-analyses suggest only 16 to 30% of them have symptoms specifically triggered by gluten [5]. The authors note that differences between studies, such as fermentable carbohydrates in the challenge foods, limit how far this can be interpreted [5].

So what else could be going on? Two explanations stand out.

**Fructans and other FODMAPs.** Foods with gluten often contain fructans, a type of FODMAP (fermentable carbohydrates that some people absorb poorly) [6]. In a Norwegian trial, 59 people on a self-chosen gluten-free diet, with coeliac disease ruled out, ate muesli bars containing gluten, fructans or placebo for 7 days each, without knowing which [6]. Fructans caused more symptoms and more bloating than gluten, and there was no difference in symptoms between gluten and placebo [6]. An earlier Australian trial in 37 people with gluten sensitivity and IBS found that symptoms improved overall on a reduced-FODMAP diet, and gluten-specific effects were seen in only 8% [7]. Both trials were small, so they cannot prove what is happening for any one person.

**Expectation.** A nocebo effect, where expecting a food to harm you produces real symptoms, has been demonstrated in wheat sensitivity [4]. The Lancet review concludes that fermentable carbohydrates and nocebo effects contribute considerably to symptoms in many cases [5].

None of this means the symptoms are imaginary. The European guideline accepts wheat sensitivity as a possible diagnosis when symptoms are reproducible and other causes have been ruled out [4]. What is unclear is which part of wheat is responsible: besides gluten and fructans, there is suggestive evidence for certain wheat proteins [4]. There is no biomarker, so it remains a diagnosis of exclusion [5]. Experts have proposed a double-blind test in which, after a gluten-free period, you take gluten and a placebo for a week each, with a symptom change of at least 30% counting as positive [8].

## What to do if coeliac disease has been ruled out

If coeliac disease and wheat allergy have been ruled out by a doctor and you still react to wheat, a structured approach is more useful than avoiding gluten for good:

1. **Keep a food and symptom diary.** Note what you eat, how much, and how you feel, including symptoms that come hours later. Fennly can help with this: it keeps gluten and fructans as separate trigger categories, allows for delayed reactions of up to 48 hours for these slow triggers, and offers a guided elimination and reintroduction test with Fennly Pro. It points to likely suspects, explicitly not a diagnosis, and it does not replace the coeliac test that comes first.
2. **Look beyond gluten.** Because fructans played a bigger role than gluten in the trials above [6] [7], a short, supervised low FODMAP phase may be worth discussing with a dietitian. It should always be followed by reintroduction; our [IBS diet guide](/en/guides/ibs-diet/) explains how that works.
3. **Do not swap one restriction for another for life.** The Lancet review advises that management should balance dietary changes with attention to psychological factors, while making sure your diet stays nutritionally adequate [5].

## When to see a doctor

See your GP and ask about a coeliac test if you have persistent unexplained gut symptoms, prolonged tiredness, weight loss you cannot explain, or a deficiency of iron, vitamin B12 or folate, or if a parent, brother, sister or child has coeliac disease [2] [3]. The full list of warning signs is below this article. If your lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or you struggle to breathe after eating, that can be a serious allergic reaction: call 999 in the UK, 911 in the US, or your local emergency number [11].
