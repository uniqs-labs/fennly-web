Diarrhea after eating is common and often harmless: a stomach bug or food poisoning usually settles within a week, and a food intolerance can cause loose stools a few hours after a meal [1] [2] [5]. If it happens straight after eating again and again, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is one possible reason [6]. Food intolerances such as lactose or fructose often show up hours later, and sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, caffeine or alcohol are also worth checking [2] [4] [5]. An often overlooked cause is bile acid diarrhea [4]. Blood in your stool, severe pain, signs of dehydration or diarrhea that lasts 4 weeks or more belong with a doctor [2] [3] [4].

## When is diarrhea after eating a reason to see a doctor?

Most diarrhea is short-lived. The NHS says it usually stops within 5 to 7 days in adults, and the most common causes are a stomach bug or food poisoning [1]. Doctors call diarrhea chronic when it lasts at least 4 weeks [3], and the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) says symptoms lasting longer than 4 weeks suggest a cause other than an infection and should be looked into [4].

Call 999 in the UK (911 in the US, otherwise your local emergency number) or go to A&E if you have a sudden, severe tummy ache, vomit blood or vomit that looks like ground coffee, are having severe difficulty breathing or are confused or not responding as usual [1].

Get medical advice soon (in the UK, NHS 111) if [1] [2]:

- you have bloody diarrhea, black and tarry stools, or bleeding from your bottom
- you have signs of dehydration, such as extreme thirst, dizziness, dark pee or peeing less than usual
- you keep being sick and cannot keep fluids down
- you have a high fever or six or more loose stools a day
- diarrhea has lasted more than 7 days

The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) is stricter and advises adults to talk with a doctor right away if diarrhea lasts more than 2 days [2]. The NHS also advises an urgent appointment for IBS-like symptoms with unexplained weight loss or a hard lump in your tummy [6].

## Diarrhea right after eating: IBS and a busy bowel

Eating sets your bowel in motion. In a small study of 12 healthy people, a large meal and a cup of caffeinated coffee each clearly increased activity in the large intestine [10]. With IBS, the bowel tends to react more strongly: the NHS lists diarrhea, sometimes with a sudden need to poo, as a main symptom, along with stomach pain or cramps that are usually worse after eating and better after a bowel movement [6]. NICE adds that urgency is more common in diarrhea-predominant IBS [7].

The NHS names alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety and regular antibiotic use as possible triggers of IBS flare-ups, while noting that flare-ups can also happen for no obvious reason [6]. What guidelines advise eating with IBS is covered in our [IBS diet guide](/en/guides/ibs-diet/).

A rarer cause of diarrhea within 30 minutes of a meal is early dumping syndrome. Food moves too quickly from the stomach into the small intestine, and diarrhea, cramps, nausea, flushing, sweating or a fast heartbeat can follow [12]. The most common cause is surgery on the stomach or oesophagus [12], so if you have had such an operation and notice this pattern, tell the team that treats you.

## Diarrhea hours after eating: food intolerances

With a food intolerance, the NHS says symptoms usually start a few hours after eating, and they can last for hours or days. Diarrhea, bloating, wind and tummy pain are the most common symptoms [5]. That delay is why the last thing you ate is not always the culprit: diarrhea in the evening can come from lunch.

### Lactose

Lactose intolerance is the most common food intolerance [5]. Undigested milk sugar reaches the large intestine, where bacteria break it down and produce fluid and gas [11]. Symptoms such as diarrhea, bloating and wind may start within a few hours of milk or dairy, and they can be mild or severe depending on how much lactose you had [11]. How much lactose most people still tolerate is explained in our [lactose intolerance guide](/en/guides/lactose-intolerance/).

### Fructose

Fruit sugar is found in fruit, fruit juice and honey and is added to many foods and soft drinks; in dietary fructose intolerance it may cause diarrhea [2]. NICE advises people with IBS to have no more than 3 portions of fresh fruit a day [7]. Keep this apart from hereditary fructose intolerance, a rare inherited condition that starts in infancy and can damage the liver and kidneys [15]. If you have always vomited or felt unwell after fruit, juice or sweet foods, do not test fructose on yourself: see a doctor.

### Sorbitol and other sugar alcohols

Sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, mannitol and xylitol are used in sugar-free sweets and chewing gum and may cause diarrhea in some people [2]. They pass into the large intestine unchanged and can cause diarrhea if taken in large quantities, for example in diet drinks or gum [4]. The effect is known well enough to be on labels: in the EU and the UK, foods with more than 10% added polyols must say that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects [14]. In the US, the label must carry a laxative warning if a food could lead to a daily intake of 50 g of sorbitol [13]. NICE advises people with IBS and diarrhea to avoid sorbitol [7].

## Coffee, alcohol, fat and spicy food

The BSG lists caffeine (in coffee, cola and energy drinks), excess alcohol and artificial sweeteners among the dietary causes of chronic diarrhea that doctors should ask about [4]. In the coffee study above, caffeinated coffee stimulated the large intestine about as much as a meal and 60% more than water [10]. That was 12 healthy volunteers, so it explains a mechanism rather than predicting how you will react.

For IBS, NICE advises limiting tea and coffee to 3 cups a day and cutting down on alcohol and fizzy drinks [7]. Spicy and fatty foods are named by the NHS as possible IBS triggers [6], and during a bout of diarrhea it may help to avoid fatty or spicy food and fruit juice or fizzy drinks [1]. None of this means these foods are off limits for good. They are suspects to check, one at a time.

## Bile acid diarrhea: an often overlooked cause

Bile acids are needed to digest fat. Your liver makes them, and up to 95% are normally reabsorbed and reused [4]. In bile acid diarrhea, more of them are lost than usual; the standard SeHCAT scan measures how much the body retains over 7 days [4]. The BSG estimates that bile acid diarrhea is about as common as coeliac disease and that up to 30% of people with diarrhea-predominant IBS show signs of it on testing [4].

It is more likely after some operations. After surgery on the end of the small intestine, bile acid diarrhea typically occurs after meals, and chronic diarrhea may occur in up to 10% of people after gallbladder removal [4].

The tests are where guidelines disagree. The BSG recommends a positive diagnosis with a SeHCAT scan or a blood test (C4) for people with functional diarrhea or diarrhea-predominant IBS [4]. NICE found the evidence on SeHCAT too limited to recommend it for routine use, although centres already using it may continue [9]. In practice, it is worth asking about bile acid diarrhea if your diarrhea is persistent and other causes have been ruled out. Treatment belongs with your doctor.

## What you can do yourself

### Rule out coeliac disease first

The BSG lists coeliac disease among the common causes of chronic diarrhea, and diarrhea is reported in 43% to 85% of people newly diagnosed with it [4]. NICE recommends offering a blood test to people with persistent unexplained gut symptoms [8]. The test only works while you are eating gluten: some gluten in more than 1 meal every day for at least 6 weeks beforehand. Do not go gluten-free until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis [8].

### Find your pattern instead of cutting out everything

The NHS warns against cutting foods out of your diet without advice from a GP or dietitian, because you could miss out on vitamins and minerals [5]. Instead, a food and symptom diary can help you and your doctor work out what triggers your symptoms [5]. For diarrhea, note when it starts relative to the meal, how much you ate, and what you drank, including coffee, alcohol and sugar-free products. ### Test a suspect for a limited time

Once a food looks suspicious, the usual next step is a time-limited test: you avoid it for a few weeks, then eat it again to see whether the symptoms come back, ideally with a dietitian [5]. If cutting out lactose clearly helps, that supports lactose as a trigger; the BSG names withdrawing lactose from the diet as one option besides a breath test [4]. Avoid stacking several eliminations at once, so you can still tell which change made the difference.

## Which tests make sense?

It depends on the pattern. For suspected lactose intolerance, a hydrogen breath test is an option [4] [5]. For chronic diarrhea, the BSG recommends that first tests, including a blood test for coeliac disease and a stool test for inflammation (faecal calprotectin), are done in primary care before referral to a specialist [4]. Further steps such as a colonoscopy or tests for bile acid diarrhea depend on your age, your symptoms and what the first results show [4].

The NHS does not recommend some home tests that claim to diagnose food intolerances: there is limited evidence that they give accurate results, and they may push you to avoid many foods, which can be harmful [5].
