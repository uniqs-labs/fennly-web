Bearable and Fennly both help you link what you do with how you feel, but they are built for different jobs. Bearable is a broad symptom and mood tracker for any chronic health issue, where you can log almost anything, from medication and sleep to meals, and look for correlations [1] [5]. Fennly is a food and symptom diary: it breaks each meal into ingredients, assigns them to 14 trigger categories and shows likely suspects after a few days. If you track several conditions, mood or medication, Bearable is likely the better fit; if your main question is which foods are behind your gut symptoms, Fennly is built for that.

## What Bearable does well

Bearable's biggest strength is its breadth. You can track mood, emotions, symptoms of any health condition, medication, nutrition and meals, hydration, menstrual cycle, physical activity, bowel movements, energy, sleep, and health measurements such as steps, heart rate and blood pressure [1]. It syncs health data from Apple Health and Google Fit and has a direct Fitbit integration [1]. Almost everything is customisable, from symptoms and symptom groups to factors, meals, medications and goals [1]. If you live with more than one condition, that flexibility is a real strength.

The free version is generous: according to the website you can track an unlimited number of custom symptoms, moods and health metrics without paying, and you get weekly reports and a graph to compare factors with your health [1]. Bearable also offers reminders for entries and medication, and export features for doctor or therapist appointments [1] [5].

On privacy, Bearable says it does not and will never sell personal data, encrypts health data before it is backed up on its servers, does not ask for your name, age, sex or gender, and lets you export and delete your data in the app [1].

We also like how honest its support pages are about statistics. Bearable explains that it can find patterns between symptoms and factors, but cannot tell you whether a factor caused the change, and it names reverse relationships, confounding variables and outliers as common pitfalls [4]. That is exactly the right warning for any symptom diary.

## How Bearable finds patterns

Bearable compares days when a factor happened with days when it did not. You start to see correlations after at least 3 days with a factor and 3 days without it, with a mood, symptom, sleep or energy score on those days [2]. Its troubleshooting page suggests that at least 7 days with and 7 days without a factor will help, and the support team recommends tracking a factor over a 30 day period [2] [4].

Timing is part of the picture. Bearable says it takes timing into account, so it would not link, for example, caffeine in the evening with symptoms logged in the morning [2]. You can log symptoms and factors in time periods of the day, and the Factor Effect report shows effects from the same day up to a seven day window after a factor, which can reveal delayed effects [3] [4]. With a free account, the main tool for this is the comparison graph; the automated Factor Effect reports come with the free trial or the subscription [2].

For food, this means you decide what counts as a factor. You might log "dairy", "onion" or "coffee" as separate items, because the support pages advise tracking only the factors you suspect most [2]. The pages we read describe comparing days with and without a factor; they do not describe breaking meals into ingredients or weighting correlations by how much of a food you ate.

## What Fennly does differently

Fennly is narrower on purpose. It is built for one question: which foods are the most likely suspects behind symptoms such as bloating, tummy pain or diarrhoea? You log a meal by photo, voice or text, and the AI breaks the dish into ingredients and assigns them to 14 trigger categories: lactose, fructose, sorbitol, mannitol, fructans, GOS, histamine, gluten, caffeine, alcohol, capsaicin, egg, soy and sulphites. So a pasta dish with a creamy garlic sauce is not one item, but lactose and fructans you can compare across days.

In the evening you do a quick day check: one tap on a calm day, a slider on a bad one, plus acute entries when something happens. The analysis looks at the whole day, and for slow triggers such as gluten, fructans and GOS it looks back up to 48 hours. It counts how much of a trigger you had over the day rather than just yes or no, and it takes stress and sleep into account. After a few days, Fennly shows suspects with a rating of how confident it is. With Fennly Pro you get a guided elimination and reintroduction test and a comparison of days with and without a suspect.

What Fennly is not belongs here too. Fennly is not a medical device and does not give a diagnosis. Its day check can include mood, but Fennly is built around meals, not as an all-in-one tracker for many conditions like Bearable. What it shows you is a suspicion to discuss with a GP or dietitian.

## Which app fits your situation?

### You track several conditions, mood or medication

Choose Bearable. It is made for tracking many things at once, including mental health, pain, fatigue, medication and sleep, and for preparing for appointments with doctors and therapists [1] [5]. If you also want to see whether meals play a role, you can add them as factors.

### Your main problem is symptoms after eating

Fennly is the more focused tool. The NHS says food intolerance symptoms usually start a few hours after eating and can last for a few hours or days [7], which is why a diary that looks back over the previous day helps. Breaking meals into trigger categories saves you from guessing which ingredient in a dish to log. How to set up any food diary so it shows patterns is covered in our guide to [keeping a food diary](/en/guides/food-diary/).

### You have IBS

Both can be useful. The NHS suggests that people with IBS keep a diary of what they eat and any symptoms they get, and try to avoid things that trigger their IBS [8]. If food is your main suspect, a food-focused diary fits; if your IBS comes with other symptoms you want to follow, Bearable's breadth may suit you better. Our guide to the [IBS diet](/en/guides/ibs-diet/) explains what the guidelines recommend.

## Whichever app you choose

Neither app can diagnose a food intolerance. The NHS says a GP may refer you to a dietitian and that you may be asked to keep a food and symptoms diary to work out what may be triggering your symptoms [7]. It also warns that some tests you can buy that claim to diagnose food intolerances are not recommended, because there is limited evidence that they give accurate results [7]. Which tests make sense is covered in our guide to [food intolerance tests](/en/guides/food-intolerance-test/).

Be careful about what you do with a suspicion. The NHS advises not to cut foods out of your diet without the advice of a GP or dietitian, because you could miss out on certain vitamins and minerals [7]. For IBS, NICE says advice on single food avoidance and exclusion diets, such as a low FODMAP diet, should only be given by a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [9]. Plan any elimination as a time-limited phase followed by reintroduction, not as a permanent list of banned foods; the NHS describes avoiding a food for a few weeks and then eating it again to see if symptoms return [7].

If gluten comes up as a suspect, get tested for coeliac disease before you go gluten free. NICE explains that tests are only accurate while you are eating gluten, and advises eating some gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing [10]. And if your symptoms keep coming back, see a GP [7].

## Our verdict

Bearable is a strong, flexible choice if you want one place for symptoms, mood, sleep, medication and habits across one or more conditions, with a free version that already covers a lot [1]. Its support pages are also refreshingly clear that a correlation is not proof [4].

Fennly fits better if your question is narrower: which foods are the most likely suspects behind your gut symptoms. It does the ingredient work for you, considers delayed reactions and amounts, and shows suspects rather than a diagnosis. Whichever you use, take what you find to a GP or dietitian before you change your diet for good.
